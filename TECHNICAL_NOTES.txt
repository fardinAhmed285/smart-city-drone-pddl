1. DOMAIN SPECIFICATION AND PDDL MODELING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.1 Typed Object Hierarchy
The domain enforces strong typing using the :typing requirement. To prevent VAL's 
parser from cyclically treating primitive domain types as subtypes of supply, the 
hierarchy explicitly anchors primary entities to object:

  (:types
    drone location supply level - object
    medkit water comms - supply
  )

  - drone: Autonomous aerial vehicles carrying out delivery, inspection, and 
    reconnaissance missions.
  - location: Discrete spatial nodes in the smart city (depots, charging stations, 
    mission disaster sites).
  - supply: Emergency payloads required at disaster sites. Specialized subtypes 
    (medkit, water, comms) provide clear semantics for heterogeneous emergency goods.
  - level: Symbolic discrete battery levels (b0 through b6).

1.2 Predicate Set and Semantics
The predicates capture all dynamic and static aspects of the smart-city emergency 
operations without relying on unsupported numeric fluents:

  ;; Drone state
  (at ?d - drone ?l - location)
    Asserts that drone ?d is currently positioned at location ?l.
  (empty ?d - drone)
    Indicates drone ?d is carrying no supply payload (payload capacity = 1).
  (carrying ?d - drone ?s - supply)
    Indicates drone ?d is actively transporting supply package ?s.
  (battery ?d - drone ?l - level)
    Tracks the exact discrete battery charge level ?l of drone ?d.

  ;; Topology & Accessibility
  (connected ?from ?to - location)
    Static graph adjacency. Declared symmetrically in :init for all traversable edges.
  (accessible ?l - location)
    Dynamic reachability flag. Drones can only fly to accessible locations. 
    Locked sites lack this fact initially until unlocked by prerequisite events.

  ;; Unlocking Dependencies
  (inspected ?l - location)
    Indicates location ?l has undergone on-site aerial sensor inspection.
  (unlocked-by-inspection ?source ?target - location)
    Static rule declaring that inspecting ?source dynamically unlocks ?target.
  (unlocked-by-delivery ?source ?target - location)
    Static rule declaring that delivering needed emergency relief to ?source unlocks ?target.

  ;; Supply Demand & Fulfilment
  (supply-at ?s - supply ?l - location)
    Indicates physical ground presence of supply ?s at location ?l.
  (needs ?l - location ?s - supply)
    Specifies that location ?l requires a specific emergency package ?s.
  (delivered ?s - supply ?l - location)
    Goal achievement fact: supply package ?s has been successfully delivered to ?l.

  ;; Infrastructure & Symbolic Arithmetic
  (has-charging-station ?l - location)
    Identifies fixed ground charging pads.
  (flight-cost ?from ?to - location ?c - level)
    Static lookup for edge traversal energy consumption in battery levels.
  (battery-after ?before ?c ?left - level)
    Symbolic subtraction relation (?before - ?c = ?left), defined only for ?before >= ?c.
    Eliminates invalid flights with negative battery without requiring numeric inequalities.
  (next-level ?l1 ?l2 - level)
    Symbolic successor relation (?l2 = ?l1 + 1) used by Model B's incremental charging.
  (max-level ?l - level)
    Identifies maximum battery capacity for Model A's instant full recharge.

1.3 Metric and Action Costs
Because Fast Downward does not support numeric state fluents (decrease/increase of 
battery fluents), plan cost is formulated via the standard :action-costs requirement:
  - (= (total-cost) 0) initialized in :init.
  - (:metric minimize (total-cost)) in all problems.
  - Flights increase total-cost by the static function (distance ?from ?to).
  - Every non-flight action (inspect, pick-up-supply, deliver-supply, recharge) 
    incurs a flat action cost of 1.
  - Parameter names avoid reserved PDDL keywords (e.g., ?left is used instead of ?after).

1.4 Action Preconditions and Effects
  - fly(?d ?from ?to ?before ?c ?left):
      Preconditions: (at ?d ?from), (connected ?from ?to), (accessible ?to),
                     (battery ?d ?before), (flight-cost ?from ?to ?c),
                     (battery-after ?before ?c ?left).
      Effects: not (at ?d ?from), (at ?d ?to), not (battery ?d ?before),
               (battery ?d ?left), (increase (total-cost) (distance ?from ?to)).
  - inspect(?d ?l):
      Preconditions: (at ?d ?l), not (inspected ?l).
      Effects: (inspected ?l), conditional effect forall ?y when (unlocked-by-inspection ?l ?y) -> (accessible ?y),
               (increase (total-cost) 1).
  - pick-up-supply(?d ?s ?l):
      Preconditions: (at ?d ?l), (supply-at ?s ?l), (empty ?d).
      Effects: not (supply-at ?s ?l), not (empty ?d), (carrying ?d ?s),
               (increase (total-cost) 1).
  - deliver-supply(?d ?s ?l):
      Preconditions: (at ?d ?l), (carrying ?d ?s), (needs ?l ?s).
      Effects: not (carrying ?d ?s), (empty ?d), (delivered ?s ?l), (supply-at ?s ?l),
               not (needs ?l ?s), conditional effect forall ?y when (unlocked-by-delivery ?l ?y) -> (accessible ?y),
               (increase (total-cost) 1).
  - recharge(?d ?l ...):
      Model A: sets battery directly to max-level (?max).
      Model B: sets battery to next-level (?next), requiring iterative execution.


2. COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS OF RECHARGE MODELS (MODEL A vs. MODEL B)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The two domain models represent a fundamental modeling trade-off in AI planning:

Dimension 1: Expressiveness and Realism
  - Model A represents an idealized battery swap or instant-recharge abstraction. 
    It assumes that any visit to a charging pad fully replenishes the battery in 
    a single unit of operational time/cost. While suitable for high-level logistics 
    where recharging overhead is lumped into a single step, it fails to capture partial 
    top-offs or time-dependent charging curves.
  - Model B models charging as discrete energy increments via (next-level ?cur ?next). 
    This allows a drone to recharge only as much as needed for its next leg (e.g., 
    topping up 1 or 2 levels instead of all 6). This is far more realistic for 
    time-sensitive emergency response, where overcharging wastes critical mission time.

Dimension 2: Planning Complexity and State Space
  - Model A yields a significantly smaller and more tractable state space. At a charging 
    station, a drone has exactly one deterministic recharge transition: cur -> max. 
    This creates an immediate transition to full autonomy, pruning intermediate charge states.
  - Model B introduces a higher branching factor and longer solution horizons. A drone at 
    a charging station can choose to recharge once, twice, or up to the maximum capacity, 
    or depart immediately. In optimal heuristic search (A* with LM-cut), every partial charge 
    level represents a distinct reachable state, multiplying the search graph size.

Dimension 3: Plan Quality and Runtime
  - In small instances, Model A plans have fewer actions because full charge is obtained 
    in 1 step (plan cost 11 vs 12).
  - Under A* search, Model B requires expanding substantially more states to prove 
    optimality (e.g., in Medium, Model A expanded 184,238 states in 38.58s with cost 32; 
    Model B expanded 446,518 states in 68.12s with cost 38).
  - However, in situations where only 1 unit of energy is required to reach a nearby depot, 
    Model B can avoid charging up to max if the flight distance allows it, though each 
    recharge step adds an action cost of 1.

Dimension 4: Modeling Trade-Offs
  - Model A trades physical realism for planning efficiency, making it the preferred 
    abstraction for large multi-agent fleets where search complexity dominates.
  - Model B trades search tractability for operational fidelity, allowing fine-grained 
    energy scheduling at the expense of exponential state space growth.


3. PROBLEM INSTANCE DESIGN AND DESIGN CONSTRAINT SATISFACTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.1 Problem 1: Small (problem-small.pddl)
  - Layout: 1 drone (drone1), 5 locations: depot, charging, site1, site2, site3.
  - Battery capacity: 4 levels (b0 to b4). Unit edge costs (distance 1, flight-cost b1).
  - Goals: (inspected site1), (inspected site2), (delivered med1 site3).
  - Unavoidable Recharging Proof:
      The topology forms a star graph centered at the charging station:
        depot <-> charging <-> site1
                           <-> site2
                           <-> site3
      Drone1 starts at depot with med1. To achieve all goals:
        1. Must leave depot: depot -> charging (1 flight, battery = 3).
        2. To visit any site: charging -> site (1 flight, battery = 2).
        3. To visit the next site, it must return through charging: site -> charging (1 flight, battery = 1).
        4. To reach the second site: charging -> site (1 flight, battery = 0).
      At this point, 4 flights have occurred, battery is b0, and the drone is stranded at the second site.
      The third site remains unvisited. Visiting all 3 mission sites requires a minimum of 6 flights 
      (depot->ch->siteA->ch->siteB->ch->siteC = 6 flights).
      Because maximum battery capacity is 4 levels, completion without recharging is mathematically impossible.

3.2 Problem 2: Medium (problem-medium.pddl)
  - Layout: 2 drones (drone1 at depot, drone2 at ch2), 8 locations (depot, ch1, ch2, site1-site5).
  - Battery capacity: 6 levels (b0 to b6). Flight costs vary between 1 and 2.
  - Goals: 4 inspections (site1, site2, site3, site4); 3 deliveries across 2 supply types:
      med1 -> site1, med2 -> site3, water1 -> site5.
  - Locked Location Verification:
      Site5 is initially locked (no (accessible site5) fact in :init).
      Site4 and Site5 are adjacent. Inspecting site4 triggers the conditional effect:
        (when (unlocked-by-inspection site4 site5) (accessible site5))
      Failed Action Sequence Proof:
        If drone2 attempts to fly directly to site5 from ch2:
          (fly drone2 ch2 site5 b6 b2 b4)
        The action precondition (accessible site5) evaluates to FALSE in the initial state,
        blocking direct entry. The planner is forced to dispatch an inspection drone to site4 
        prior to entering site5.

3.3 Problem 3: Large (problem-large.pddl) and Single-Drone Variant (problem-large-1drone.pddl)
  - Layout: 11 locations (depot_west, depot_east, ch_west, ch_east, site_w1, site_w2, site_w3, 
    site_e1, site_e2, site_e3, site_e4).
  - Supplies: medkit (med1, med2), water (water1), comms (comm1) - 3 distinct types.
  - Goals (7 total):
      Inspections: site_w1, site_e1, site_e3.
      Deliveries: med1 -> site_w2, water1 -> site_w3, med2 -> site_e2, comm1 -> site_e4.
  - Delivery Unlocking Dependency:
      Site_e4 is initially locked. Delivering med2 to site_e2 triggers:
        (when (unlocked-by-delivery site_e2 site_e4) (accessible site_e4))
      This models an emergency disaster zone where communication deployment (comm1) cannot 
      proceed until critical medical relief (med2) has cleared the route.
  - Single-Drone vs. Multi-Drone Comparative Advantage:
      The map is geographically partitioned into a West Sector and an East Sector, separated by 
      a high-cost transit corridor (ch_west <-> ch_east, distance 3, flight cost b3).
      In problem-large.pddl (3 drones):
        - drone1 is stationed at depot_west and services West mission sites locally.
        - drone2 and drone3 are stationed in the East Sector (ch_east and depot_east) and service 
          East mission sites locally.
        - No drone ever needs to cross the expensive inter-sector corridor! Cross-corridor transit cost = 0.
      In problem-large-1drone.pddl (1 drone):
        - Only drone1 is deployed, starting at depot_west.
        - To satisfy East Sector goals, drone1 MUST make multiple round-trip traversals across the 
          corridor (cost 3 per crossing), incurring severe flight penalties and requiring frequent 
          recharge stops (adding recharge action costs).
      This topological design guarantees a substantial, measurable cost reduction for the 3-drone fleet.


4. LITERATURE CONNECTION (ACADEMIC REPORT CITATIONS)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Paper 1: Autonomous UAV Mission Planning with Consumable Resources
Citation:
  Bernardini, S., Fox, M., & Long, D. (2014). Planning the Autonomous Flight 
  of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles. IEEE Intelligent Systems, 29(2), 26-33.
Connection to Project Decisions:
  Bernardini et al. investigate automated task and trajectory planning for 
  unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) performing multi-target surveillance and 
  relief delivery under strict onboard energy and payload constraints. Their 
  findings show that battery consumption acts as a primary pruning frontier, 
  requiring planners to tightly coordinate spatial movement with scheduled visits 
  to designated recharging infrastructure. In our project, this directly motivates 
  our discrete symbolic battery encoding (levels b0 through b6) and the explicit 
  subtraction relation (battery-after ?before ?c ?left). Because classical 
  Fast Downward search does not support continuous numeric fluents, adopting 
  symbolic level transitions provides the exact mathematical bounding needed to 
  prevent drones from attempting flights beyond their physical capacity, aligning 
  with the resource discretization principles established by Bernardini et al.

Paper 2: Admissible Landmark Heuristics for Optimal Logistics Planning
Citation:
  Helmert, M., & Domshlak, C. (2009). Landmarks, Critical Paths and Abstractions: 
  What's the Difference Anyway? In Proceedings of the 19th International 
  Conference on Automated Planning and Scheduling (ICAPS 2009), pp. 162-169.
Connection to Project Decisions:
  Helmert and Domshlak introduced the landmark-cut (LM-cut) heuristic, an 
  admissible cost-partitioning heuristic that computes tight lower bounds by finding 
  minimal disjunctive action sets (landmarks) in relaxed planning graphs. In our 
  empirical evaluation, A* with LM-cut serves as the gold standard for cost-optimal 
  emergency dispatch, successfully establishing proven lower-bound optimal plan costs 
  (e.g., cost 11 on Small and cost 32 on Medium under Model A). However, our 
  experimental results also illustrate the inherent computational trade-off identified 
  by Helmert and Domshlak: because LM-cut calculates justification graphs and cut 
  sets at every search node, the search runtime per state is substantial. On larger 
  instances, where multiple drones multiply the state branching factor, A* with LM-cut 
  experiences exponential state expansion and reaches timeout limits, contrasting 
  sharply with the fast greedy satisficing search of LAMA-first.

6. ANALYTICAL DISCUSSION PER PROBLEM INSTANCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.1 Small Instance Analysis
The Small instance posed minimal difficulty for both planners, with all configurations 
terminating in under 0.6 seconds. Fast Downward's translator instantiated only 41 SAS+ 
operators and 7 variables. Because maximum battery capacity was restricted to b4 and 
the tour requires 6 flights, the search space quickly forced the drone to the charging 
station. Under Model A, A* with LM-cut found the optimal 11-step, cost-11 plan after 
expanding only 44 states. Model B required expanding 68 states to reach the optimal 
cost-12 plan (12 steps). The cost difference of 1 directly reflects Model B's incremental 
recharging: instead of one recharge action to full capacity (b1 -> b4 costing 1 in Model A), 
the drone executed two incremental recharge actions (costing 2 in Model B). This validates 
that even on toy instances, Model B incurs additional action costs proportional to the 
discretization steps.

6.2 Medium Instance Analysis
The Medium instance clearly exposed the divergence between satisficing greedy search 
and optimal admissible search. LAMA-first solved both models in under 0.8 seconds, 
producing satisficing plans of cost 42 (Model A, 35 steps) and cost 55 (Model B, 48 steps). 
However, proving optimality with A* and LM-cut required significant search effort: 
Model A required 184,238 expanded states and 38.60 seconds to find the optimal plan of cost 32 
(28 steps, a 23.8% cost improvement over LAMA-first). In Model B, the expansion expanded 
more than 2.4-fold to 446,518 states and 68.12 seconds, arriving at an optimal cost of 38. 
The difficulty arose from the combination of multi-drone synchronization (coordinating 
drone1 at depot and drone2 at ch2) and the neighboring unlocking constraint on site5. 
The discrete battery encoding dramatically multiplied the state space in Model B because 
at every charging visit, each discrete intermediate battery level (b0 through b6) created 
a distinct node in the state graph.

6.3 Large Instance (3 Drones) Analysis
On the 3-drone Large instance, A* with LM-cut completely timed out (> 300 s) on both 
Domain Models. The difficulty stemmed from the combinatorial explosion of the joint 
state space: 3 autonomous drones, each possessing independent spatial locations, 7 battery 
levels, and cargo carrying statuses across 11 map nodes, yielding over 590 ground SAS+ operators. 
Computing the LM-cut landmark graph and relaxed planning cuts at each state node proved 
prohibitively expensive. Conversely, LAMA-first navigated this vast search space with ease, 
returning valid plans in 0.70 seconds (Model A, cost 35, 32 steps) and 0.68 seconds 
(Model B, cost 61, 52 steps). When allowed to run in anytime mode, LAMA progressively 
pruned and optimized the plans, reducing Model A's cost from 35 down to 30 (29 steps) 
and Model B's cost from 61 down to 31 (40 steps).

6.4 Large Single-Drone Variant Analysis & Fleet Comparison
In stark contrast to the 3-drone setup, the single-drone variant was solved to proven 
optimality by A* with LM-cut in just 1.16 seconds (Model A, cost 39, 4,615 expanded states) 
and 1.35 seconds (Model B, cost 53, 7,529 expanded states). Collapsing the fleet to a single 
drone eliminated multi-agent interleaving, reducing the state space dimensionality to a 
fraction of the 3-drone model.

Cost Comparison and Fleet Advantage Quantification:
  1. Under Cost-Optimized / Anytime LAMA (Model A):
     - Single-Drone Best Validated Cost: 39
     - Three-Drone Best Validated Cost: 30
     - Absolute Cost Reduction: 39 - 30 = 9 units
     - Percentage Cost Reduction: (9 / 39) * 100% = 23.08%

  2. Under Satisficing LAMA-First (Model A):
     - Single-Drone Validated Cost: 56
     - Three-Drone Validated Cost: 35
     - Absolute Cost Reduction: 56 - 35 = 21 units
     - Percentage Cost Reduction: (21 / 56) * 100% = 37.50%

  3. Under Cost-Optimized / Anytime LAMA (Model B):
     - Single-Drone Best Validated Cost: 53
     - Three-Drone Best Validated Cost: 31
     - Absolute Cost Reduction: 53 - 31 = 22 units
     - Percentage Cost Reduction: (22 / 53) * 100% = 41.51%

Why Multiple Drones Reduce Total Mission Cost:
The significant cost reduction achieved by deploying 3 drones is directly explained by 
the topological design of our smart-city environment. The city is partitioned into 
West and East sectors separated by a long, expensive transit corridor (ch_west <-> ch_east) 
with an edge distance of 3 and flight energy cost of b3.
In the 3-drone fleet, drones are pre-positioned across both sectors (drone1 at depot_west, 
drone2 at ch_east, drone3 at depot_east). Each drone services its local sector: drone1 
fulfills all West deliveries and inspections, while drone2 and drone3 service the East. 
Crucially, NOT A SINGLE DRONE EVER CROSSES THE INTER-SECTOR CORRIDOR. Total cross-corridor 
flight cost for the 3-drone fleet is exactly 0.

In contrast, the single drone (starting at depot_west) cannot avoid the corridor. Because 
a drone can carry only one supply at a time and four supplies must be delivered across both 
sectors, the single drone is forced to traverse the long corridor back and forth, consuming 
3 battery units per transit. Each transit drains its battery, necessitating additional 
recharging cycles (which each add an action cost of 1). Thus, multi-drone deployment avoids 
redundant spatial transit and excessive charging overhead, yielding a proven 23.08% to 41.51% 
reduction in total mission cost.
